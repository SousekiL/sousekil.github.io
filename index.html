Why does everywhere feel far away in Beijing?

Tools & methodsGaode Map POI400 m hexagonal gridsOpenStreetMapPopulation-weighted route samplingDetour index

Background

Beijing and Shanghai both have abundant urban resources, yet everyday access can feel different. The article asks whether Beijing’s large blocks, lower local service density, and less connected street network help explain why short trips often feel longer.

Method

Using 2022 Gaode POI data, the study compares Beijing within the Fifth Ring Road with Shanghai within the Outer Ring Road. It measures service density in 400-metre hexagonal cells, compares facilities around transit stations, examines population and commercial-resource distributions, and estimates a detour index from 10,000 population-weighted sampled routes using OpenStreetMap routing data.

Conclusion

Beijing’s services are more spatially dispersed, its population and commercial centers are less aligned, and its average detour index is higher than Shanghai’s in the study window. These patterns offer a data-based explanation for the feeling that destinations in Beijing are farther away. The results describe a comparison of selected urban areas, not a general ranking of the two cities.

Women’s names in ancient China

Tools & methodsCBDBMing Qing Women’s WritingsName-presence rateDescriptive statisticsCharacter-frequency analysis

Background

Historical records preserve far fewer women than men, and women who do appear are often identified through their relationships to men rather than by personal names. The article asks what a historical database reveals about women’s visibility, longevity, and naming patterns.

Method

Using the China Biographical Database, the study compares the recorded numbers and naming rates of men and women across dynasties. It examines kinship descriptions, age-at-death distributions, and the frequency of characters in women’s names, with Ming Qing Women’s Writings noted as a possible reason for stronger Qing-period coverage.

Conclusion

In the database, women are recorded much less often than men, and named women form only a small share of recorded women. The surviving names also reflect changing naming conventions across periods. These results describe the historical record and its selection effects; they are not a complete measure of women’s lives in the past.

Gaokao competition structure across provinces

Tools & methodsScore-frequency tablesOne-point gap influenceBeta distribution fittingHigh-score concentration985 opportunity ratio

Background

“The most competitive province” is difficult to define because provinces use different exam structures, scoring systems, applicant populations, and admission plans. The article treats competition as a structure involving density, high-score concentration, and access to top-university places.

Method

Using 2025 score-frequency tables, provincial undergraduate cutoffs, and collected 985 university admission plans, the study measures one-point score-gap influence rates, the share of candidates in the top score segment, and the ratio of 985 places to candidates above the undergraduate cutoff. It also fits Beta distributions to compare the shape of above-cutoff scores.

Conclusion

The results distinguish high-pressure provinces with relatively narrow top-university opportunities from high-pressure provinces with wider opportunities. Jiangsu, Anhui, Yunnan, Hebei, Jiangxi, Shandong, and Henan are presented as examples of the first structure; Shanghai, Tianjin, Guangdong, Sichuan, Chongqing, Fujian, Ningxia, and Hubei are closer to the second. The comparison concerns candidates above the undergraduate cutoff, not exam difficulty or educational quality.

Administrative borders vs. cultural boundaries

Tools & methodsDialect classificationDialect-distance measurePopulation-weighted distanceRoad-distance measureClustering analysis

Background

Administrative borders are designed for governance, while cultural boundaries develop through language, migration, settlement, and long-term contact. In China, the two often overlap imperfectly, leaving culturally connected areas divided across provinces or cities.

Method

Using dialect regions as a proxy for cultural geography, the article compares county-level dialect classifications with current administrative divisions. It then combines inter-city dialect distance with road distance to form an exploratory clustering of culturally and geographically similar cities.

Conclusion

The examples show repeated mismatches: one province can contain several cultural regions, while culturally connected areas can be separated by administrative borders. The proposed “cultural provinces” are explicitly presented as a playful analytical exercise, not as a replacement for actual administrative divisions.

China’s cultural center moves south

Tools & methodsCBDBModern-area geocodingLatitude distributionMean latitude / longitudeTGI index

Background

The article uses the geographical distribution of people who entered officialdom through the imperial examination system as one way to examine changes in China’s cultural center. It treats this as a useful perspective, not a complete definition of cultural centrality.

Method

Using CBDB, the study counts broadly defined examination-entry figures by province, city, and historical period. Missing locations are supplemented in a fixed order from recorded native place, residence, birthplace, ancestral place, and registered place; locations are then assigned to modern administrative areas. Latitude distributions, mean geographic coordinates, and local surname TGI values are used for comparison.

Conclusion

The recorded pattern moves from a northern concentration in the Sui–Tang period toward the south by the Song, with the lower Yangtze and other southern areas becoming increasingly prominent. Beijing’s later political importance shifts the estimated center northward without returning it fully across the Huai River. The author cautions that incomplete historical records and database coverage limit the conclusion.
